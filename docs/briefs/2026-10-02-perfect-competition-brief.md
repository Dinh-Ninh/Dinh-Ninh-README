\# The Problem



The farm has 64 beds available and is deciding how to allocate them among three crops (tomatoes, carrots, and mesclun) over a 36-week season. This is a one-off, irreversible planting decision under perfect competition. Fixed factors include revenue per bed for each crop, fixed costs per season of $20,000, total available beds, the number of field hours the farmer can work himself (720 hours), and the limit on temporary labor.



Three interacting constraints restrict the choice. First, the total bed cap of 64 creates a physical scarcity, so not all crops can be maxed out. Second, labor is constrained by the farmer’s available time and the expense of employing up to four temporary workers. Third, and most important, each crop exhibits diminishing returns: the greater the number of beds of a single crop that are planted, the more quickly the labor per bed grows exponentially, as the formula states:



Labor (q) = q × hrs/wk/bed × 36 × (1 + dim) ^q.



This means that marginal costs increase as the planting area increases.



\# The Hypothesis



I expect the optimal mix to be approximately 12 beds of tomatoes, 20 beds of carrots, and 32 beds of mesclun (though mesclun will be capped at 30, so likely 12 tomatoes, 20 carrots, and 30 mesclun, leaving 2 beds unplanted or reallocated). I reason that tomatoes have the highest revenue per bed ($8,800) but also the highest labor intensity (2.5 hrs/wk/bed) and the steepest diminishing-returns rate (10%), meaning their marginal cost rises very quickly. Carrots have lower revenue ($2,094) but much lower labor intensity (0.833 hrs/wk) and a low diminishing-returns rate (2.5%), making them cheap to expand. Mesclun offers moderate revenue ($2,700) and labor intensity (1.25 hrs/wk), with the lowest diminishing-returns rate (1.25%), so it can be planted extensively without incurring prohibitive labor costs.



The mechanism deciding the outcome will be the interaction between revenue per bed and the exponential labor penalty. I expect the binding constraint to be total labor hours (720 own + up to 5,760 temporary) rather than the bed cap, because the exponential labor function will make large tomato plantings prohibitively expensive in hours.



If the model shows more than 15 tomato beds or fewer than 25 mesclun beds, my Hypothesis is wrong.
